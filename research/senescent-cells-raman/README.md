## The finding (established)

Senescent cells — nicknamed "zombie cells" — stop dividing but do not die. They accumulate in tissues with age and, when they build up, contribute to inflammation and damage in surrounding tissue, including conditions such as osteoarthritis and type 2 diabetes. They are not purely harmful: senescence also plays roles in embryonic development and wound healing. The problem for researchers has been detection: the standard markers, proteins such as p16 and p21, can only be identified by staining or processing that destroys the cells, and RNA sequencing consumes them. Today, senescent cells cannot be measured in a person without a biopsy that destroys the tissue.

An MIT team has developed a non-destructive alternative, published in *Nature Aging* (September 21, 2026). Their platform, RamanOmics, combines Raman microscopy — which reads the chemical composition of cells from how they scatter light, leaving the tissue intact — with single-cell and spatial gene-expression measurements from the same cells. Tested on skin and lung tissue from young (2-month) and old (26-month) mice, the team found a recurring lipid-associated Raman signature in senescent cells shared across both organs, alongside tissue-specific aging programs: immune activity and inflammation in lung, structure and repair in skin. Machine learning combined the signals into a "barcode" that identifies senescent cells optically.

The work is preclinical: mouse tissue sections only, and no device has been built. Senior author Jeon Woong Kang (MIT's Laser Biomedical Research Center) imagines a future endoscope that could locate senescent cells inside a living body. The nearer-term value is measurement: drugs meant to clear senescent cells are being tested, and this would give researchers a repeatable way to check whether they actually work in a given patient. The study is part of the NIH Cellular Senescence Network.

## The Cloud-9 reading (interpretive)

Measurement without destruction. The cell's state is read off scattered light and the sample survives the reading — information extracted without consuming its source. The same disciplinary pattern runs through this collection: OP 313's dimming, FRB 20240304B's dispersion, and now a senescence barcode — in each case, the medium or the object betrays its state to a signal that leaves it standing. It is the precautionary principle at cellular scale: measure before you intervene, and prefer the measurement that doesn't destroy what it measures.
