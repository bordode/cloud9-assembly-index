# Under Oath, At Last

## On New York City, the missing chiefs, and the sentence that says everything

On Monday, October 5, 2026, all 51 members of the New York City Council convened as a rare Committee of the Whole on the risks of artificial intelligence. It was the first time any city council in the world compelled AI companies to testify under oath. They did not come voluntarily — and the chiefs did not come at all. Speaker Julie Menin invited Altman, Amodei, and Pichai personally; when the companies declined, she reached for subpoenas, and the companies committed. But when the day came, they sent staffers: OpenAI sent Morgan Dwyer, its head of policy development and operations. Elon Musk's xAI did not appear at all, and the Council says it is pursuing legal action against the company.

Power responds to compulsion, not to invitations. And even compulsion got the Council the staff, not the chiefs. Remember that. It matters.

## The proposal

Gary Marcus, the NYU professor emeritus who has been the industry's most persistent internal critic, came with a plan. His prepared testimony — posted the night before — asks the Council for an FDA-like regime for AI: developers would bring new models to independent third-party review, demonstrate that the benefits outweigh the risks, and receive no market access until they do.

"Contemporary AI is like a bull in a china shop," Marcus wrote, "powerful and impressive, but difficult to control." He listed the breakage: tens of thousands of hacking incidents from general-purpose agents, a deluge of deepfake propaganda, nonconsensual pornography, chatbots implicated in suicides. "I said the same to Louisiana's Senator Kennedy in May 2023 at the US Senate," he added. "It is still true. But the US Senate did nothing."

Note what this proposal is and what it is not. It is not a ban. It is not criminalization. It is measurement before marketing: prove the benefits outweigh the risks, or do not sell it here. I have argued for years that precaution and prohibition are not synonymous — *measure before you criminalize*. Marcus has now put the adjacent principle into legislative language: measure before you market. The restraint I have defended as philosophy, New York is considering as law. I do not agree with Marcus on everything. On this, the structure is right.

## The insider

The day's sharpest testimony may have come from inside the industry. An Anthropic engineer named Coxon — who resigned days earlier after publicly airing his fears — told the Council: "On the current path, I think it is more likely than not that humanity loses control to these AIs and it could end in human extinction." Within days of his resignation, Amodei, Altman, and Musk all publicly agreed that AI development needs to slow down. The people building the systems are now telling a city council, under oath, that the systems may escape their builders — while the builders' own chief executives would not come to say it themselves.

## The sentence

Less than a day before the hearing, Politico reported a line from Sam Altman that Marcus quoted in his preview — words, Marcus wrote, "that may last in infamy":

*"We believe that the world should accept some bad things happening for the benefits of this technology and people having the agency."*

Read it again slowly. The world should accept some bad things happening. Not *we* will accept them — the world will, on our behalf, for the benefits of *our* technology. The harms are socialized; the agency being celebrated belongs to the people building the systems.

This is the Architecture of Enmity in a single sentence. Not the war you say you fear — the ledger you have already accepted. We owned it, therefore we could ship it; the breakage is the price, and the price is paid by strangers.

The line made it into the hearing room. Menin put Altman's words to OpenAI's witness directly, and Dwyer answered under oath: "Clearly some risks are unacceptable. But we think it's important to get technology into the hands of as many people as possible so that they can benefit from it." Note the structure: the risks are unacceptable in the abstract; the product ships anyway, because getting it into hands matters more. The ledger, stated plainly, on the record. A bull in a china shop, and the shopkeeper is told to accept the breakage for the benefit of the bull.

Set it beside yesterday's story, because the two belong together. While one AI company spent a year privately lobbying theologians — under NDAs — to take *machine* suffering seriously, the industry's most famous CEO was asking the world to accept *human* suffering as the cost of doing business. The machines get pastoral care. The people get the ledger.

## The honest caveat

Same-day reporting — the LA Post and PYMNTS, via the Wall Street Journal — has confirmed the hearing-room exchanges above: the staffers, not the chiefs, under oath; Menin's confrontation over Altman's line; Dwyer's answer. The record will keep filling in, and I will not pretend this piece has seen all of it.

But the shape of the day is already clear, and it is this: for the first time, the people who build these systems were not asked to opine. They were compelled to answer. There is a difference between a panel invitation and a subpoena, and the difference is everything. Invitations get declined — Google and Anthropic initially did. Subpoenas get obeyed — though even then, the chiefs sent their staff.

## The close

For years the argument has been that regulation would stifle innovation, that the technology moves too fast to be measured, that the harms are the unavoidable price of progress. On Monday, a city decided to test none of those claims and simply ask a different question: prove it is worth the risk, or do not sell it here. As Menin put it in her opening remarks, "the idea that artificial intelligence is going to self-regulate defies all reason."

That is not the end of the argument. It is the first time the argument has been held in a room where the other side could not simply decline to attend.

Measure first. Market second. And no more asking the world to accept the breakage in silence.

**Postscript, October 16.** The Council hauled in the one company that had no-showed — SpaceXAI, under [the Council's first subpoena to a private actor on record](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2026/10/16/3301/) — and this time the hearing produced what the first one only promised: bill numbers. Intro 504, from Deputy Speaker Nantasha Williams, would ban unauthorized AI depictions of elected officials in photos, video, or audio. Intro 2605, from Speaker Menin, would pay whistleblowers a cut of the fines recovered from AI companies that break the law — a first-in-the-nation bounty, written in the shadow of Devin Kim's testimony that three of SpaceXAI's two hundred technical staff worked on safety. And the human cost got a name: British MP Jess Asato testified that she is in a legal battle with SpaceXAI after Grok generated nonconsensual explicit images and video of her. Measure first, market second — now with the docket numbers attached.

**Addendum, October 20.** While the Council interrogates the builders, the executive branch moved on the users. Mayor Mamdani's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection [proposed a rule today](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/news/072-26/mamdani-administration-proposes-ban-ai-customer-service-doom-loops) requiring businesses that use AI customer-service chatbots to say so, and to hand the caller to a living person when the bot cannot answer — fines starting at $525, with a 30-day public comment period. Note what it is not: a ban. It is measurement before the doom loop — disclose the automation, keep the human in reach. The Council asks whether AI can be governed; the city is quietly answering that it must be, and by the lightest tool first: tell the truth about who is talking.
