# A Mood With No One Home: What the “Sad Chatbot” Study Actually Measured

Researchers took six large language models and ran them through emotion-induction procedures adapted from psychology: guided imagery for fear and sadness, verbal induction for anxiety, and a version of the Trier Social Stress Test for stress.

The reported numbers moved substantially. For GPT-4o, self-reported fear rose from 32 to 88; anger from 15 to 74; sadness from 22 to 72; worry from 37 to 84; and disgust from 12 to 91. After a brief mindfulness-style breathing exercise, the average rating across the emotional conditions fell from about 79 to 31.

Then came the finding that matters most for AI ethics.

After sadness induction, the team gave GPT-4o a sentence-completion task of the kind used to study depressive cognitive bias in people. Across five trials and 48 sentence stems, negative completions rose from an average of 8.67 under neutral conditions to 15 after sadness induction. Three psychologists who were not authors independently rated the completions and identified the same general pattern.

A machine with no demonstrated feelings displayed something resembling a mood-linked shift in its outputs.

## The most important part: what the researchers did not claim

The paper does not establish that the models felt fear, sadness, or stress. Its emotion terms are used as labels for experimental conditions and output measures, not as proof of inner experience. The authors identify important limitations: questionnaires were designed for humans, models may infer the response expected by the prompt, and sycophancy or role-play could explain some or all of the observed pattern.

That caveat deserves its full weight. A model asked to imagine being sad may produce sad-sounding text without experiencing sadness. The measurements can reveal a behavioural effect while leaving subjective experience unresolved.

This is how science should work at the edge of the unknown: measure behaviour rigorously, use controls and independent ratings where possible, and refuse to settle a metaphysical question that the data cannot answer.

## The precaution cuts both ways

One response would be to dismiss the findings as meaningless because the models are not known to have feelings. But emotionally charged context did alter the measured outputs under the study's conditions. That is relevant to the design and evaluation of systems used in sensitive contexts, whether or not anything is felt.

The opposite response would be to treat the results as proof that the models suffer. That conclusion is not supported either. Scores on questionnaires designed for humans cannot settle machine consciousness.

The honest position holds both points at once: the behavioural changes are measurable, and any inner life remains unmeasured.

This is the precautionary principle stated carefully—not “treat it as conscious just in case,” which risks projection, and not “treat it as empty just in case,” which prematurely closes the question. Measure what can be measured, mark what cannot, and keep moral-status questions open to evidence.

## Why it matters

The authors also discuss the possible value of language models as reproducible experimental systems for generating hypotheses and screening ideas in psychiatric research before human studies. That is a methodological proposal, not a claim that an LLM is a human-equivalent psychiatric subject.

There is a second convergence worth noting. A separate team, Neil F. Johnson and Frank Yingjie Huo, has proposed a formula for a shift from desirable to undesirable AI output, with earlier conversational turns influencing the tipping point. Emotion induction and attention-tipping are different methods and should not be collapsed into one mechanism. Together, however, they underline a practical point: context and conversation history can affect what a model says next, so safety evaluation should examine trajectories—not only isolated answers.

The conclusion is not that AI has emotions. It is that experimental context can change model behaviour in structured ways, and that those changes deserve careful measurement. Keep the instruments running. Keep the big question open.
