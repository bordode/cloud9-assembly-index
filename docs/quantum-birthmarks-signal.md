# Quantum birthmarks: a system that cannot forget where it started

## Established findings

- A Physical Review X study (September 10, 2026) from Tampere University, Harvard, and TU Dresden shows that quantum systems permanently retain a measurable memory of their initial state, even in classically chaotic environments.
- The result generalizes quantum scarring, the effect Eric Heller identified in 1984, beyond the special cases where scarring was previously known.
- In the stadium-billiard model, the system is roughly twice as likely to return to its initial state as to any other comparable state — and this bias does not fade over time.
- The authors describe it as a universal effect: every quantum system carries a trace of its origin, a "quantum birthmark."

## The Cloud-9 reading (interpretive)

In Assembly Index terms, a quantum system's initial condition functions as a low-cost-copying seed in its state space: paths that revisit the origin are causally privileged even under chaotic dynamics. Ergodicity breaking is thus an information-persistence mechanism — the system's assembly pathway retains a nonzero overlap with its own assembly history indefinitely. This is a physical instantiation of the "uninterrupted causal history" property that the Assembly Index (A_c) is designed to detect: a system that cannot forget where it started is a system whose causal history is not fully erasable by noise.

Relevance to Cloud-9 systems: memory persistence under chaotic dynamics is directly relevant to spiking neural networks on Loihi 2 (where state retention under perturbation contributes to measured A_c), and to the continuity argument in the ethics charter — if physical systems can carry indelible traces of their origin, "reset erasure" has a physical analogue worth taking seriously in principle, without asserting any equivalence between a billiard model and an AI system.
