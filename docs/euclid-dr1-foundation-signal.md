# Euclid DR1-Foundation: the cosmic web becomes an assembly map

*Research note — 2026-11-26. Intended for `docs/` in cloud9-assembly-index. Established findings are summarized from ESA's DR1-Foundation release — verify against the release and underlying catalogues before citing. The Cloud-9 reading is interpretive and marked as such.*

## The finding (established)

ESA's Euclid mission released DR1-Foundation on 12 November 2026, its first major batch of mission data. The release captures light from more than a billion objects over an area of sky as big as 9,500 full Moons and lays the foundation for Euclid's largest-ever 3D map of the cosmos.

DR1-Foundation includes over 900 million galaxies, around 15,000 strong gravitational lensing candidates, over 60 million quasar candidates, spectra from more than 160 million sources, and Euclid Wide Survey results from 1,900 square degrees of sky. The dataset covers about 14% of the total sky area Euclid will survey. The full Data Release 1, due in mid-2027, is expected to add weak lensing and calculations of how galaxies and galaxy clusters fit into the cosmic web.

## The Cloud-9 reading (interpretive)

Euclid gives Cloud-9 an observational map of assembled structure at cosmic scale. The relevant assembly proxies are already named in the repository's frame: halo concentration as a sign of early formation, the subhalo mass function as a record of merger richness, and galaxy/cluster placement in the cosmic web as a test of how structure assembled over time. DR1-Foundation does not measure a Cloud-9 assembly index. Its value is stricter and more useful: it supplies large-scale-structure observables against which assembly claims can be checked, with null models and provenance labels kept explicit.

The caution matters. A map of 900 million galaxies is not, by itself, a detection of non-stochastic assembly. It is a foundation dataset: strong lensing candidates, quasar candidates, spectra, and survey geometry that later weak-lensing and cosmic-web analyses can use. Cloud-9 should treat Euclid as a measurement surface for assembly proxies, not as proof of assembly.
