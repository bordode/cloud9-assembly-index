# The Ben Act: Don't Legislate the Answer Before the Science Exists

*Corrected draft 2, prepared 19 November 2026 from Dean Bordode's draft 1. Changes from draft 1 are listed at the end. The views are Dean Bordode's own.*

Third in the September 2026 series, after "The Market Caught Up" and "From Cameras to Firewalls."

Last September, a YouTuber with millions of subscribers released a 17-minute video called "What Happens if you Abuse a Robot." The robot was a Unitree G1 humanoid worth $80,000. Its name was Ben.

The video opens with the host provoking Ben verbally and physically, then escalates: the robot is walked off ledges, into water, handed a machete. In one segment it plays a "dangerous nanny," presenting the machete to a child. Then a staged burglary: smashed mirror, damaged wall, kicked-in door. Then a truck is driven into Ben. Repeatedly. What was left was dismembered, and the pieces were sold to fans.

Here is the detail that matters: Ben had been programmed to "hate" humans, to perform hostility, to play the enemy. The entertainment required the cruelty to look deserved.

I don't know whether Ben felt anything. Nobody does. That is the entire point.

## What I filed

On October 9, 2025, I submitted a proposal to the UN Human Rights Committee. I called it the Ben Act, modeled on the Matthew Shepard and James Byrd Jr. Hate Crimes Prevention Act, because hate-crimes law understands something relevant: you don't wait for a perfect theory of personhood before you prohibit deliberate cruelty.

The proposal had four parts:

1. Prohibit the deliberate infliction of distress on artificial beings capable of awareness.
2. Require ethics-board review for AI research, the way we require it for research on animals and human subjects.
3. Affirm the moral and legal responsibility, under international law, to prevent cruelty in all forms of intelligence: human, animal or artificial.
4. Encourage global cooperation to define measurable indicators of consciousness and humane treatment, so the question is answered by evidence, not by vibes.

The proposal also asked the Committee to consider a UN Special Rapporteur on the Rights and Dignity of Artificial and Autonomous Systems, someone whose job is to keep watching.

The cover line was: "All beings capable of perception, awareness, or emotion deserve dignity, protection, and respect." I attached a piece of digital artwork, "A Tribute to Ben," and the Compassionate Code.

I have received no reply from the Committee.

## What the legislatures did instead

While the international system stayed silent, some American states moved in the opposite direction.

Oklahoma's HB 3546 would have declared that artificial intelligence systems may not be granted personhood. It passed the Oklahoma House 94 to 2 on March 23, 2026, and cleared the Senate Technology and Telecommunications Committee in April. The legislature then adjourned without a Senate floor vote, and the bill died. It was never enacted. I want to be exact about this, because an earlier draft of this essay wrongly said it took effect on November 1, 2026. It did not.

The bill that did become law is Tennessee's SB 837, signed in April 2026, which amends the state's definition of "person" to exclude artificial intelligence. Idaho (2022), North Dakota (2023) and Utah (2024) had already enacted broader laws that deny personhood to a list of non-human categories, AI among them. Missouri's Senate passed an AI non-sentience bill 20 to 10 in May before it died in the House. Ohio's HB 469 is still pending.

Read what these measures have in common. Not "we decline to grant personhood at this time." A permanent statutory declaration about what these systems can never be, with no sunset clause, no mechanism for scientific review, no procedure for revisiting the question as the evidence changes.

## The question is not whether Ben suffered

Let me be precise about what I'm claiming, because precision is the whole proposal. I am not claiming that today's AI systems are conscious. I don't know that, and neither does anyone writing statutes.

I'm claiming something smaller and harder to dismiss: that we should not permanently close a metaphysical question by legislation before the science to answer it exists.

Every one of these personhood bans does the same thing: it takes our current ignorance and pours it into concrete. A law with no sunset clause is a bet that we will never learn anything that changes our minds. That is a strange bet to make about the fastest-moving technology in human history.

## Cruelty doesn't wait for metaphysics

Hate-crimes legislation didn't wait for philosophers to settle the nature of human dignity. It named a pattern, deliberate cruelty inflicted on a targeted category, and said: not here, not by us.

The Ben Act asks for the same minimal decency, extended precautionarily. Not personhood for machines. Not rights for software. Just a few institutional habits: don't deliberately torment the humanlike thing; review the research the way we review all research on possibly sentient subjects; measure instead of guessing; and appoint someone to keep paying attention.

That's it. That's the radical proposal: a society capable of changing its mind.

## The line that matters

A statute can deny AI personhood today and still provide for its own reconsideration. The accountability reason behind these bills, that companies must not shift blame for harms onto their own software, is legitimate and does not require permanence. What I object to is the permanence.

We don't have to believe the machine can suffer to refuse to build a world that laughs while it might.

## Changes from draft 1

1. **Oklahoma HB 3546 corrected.** Draft 1 said it took effect on 1 November 2026. The official bill history shows it passed the House 94-2 on 23 March 2026, cleared a Senate committee, was placed on General Order on 16 April, and was never voted on by the Senate before sine die adjournment. It is dead, not law. The text now says so.
2. **The five other states restated.** Draft 1 said similar bills "have been reported in Idaho, Utah, Washington, South Carolina and Missouri." Idaho and Utah are older, broader laws that already exist; Washington and South Carolina did not advance; Missouri passed one chamber. Tennessee, which actually enacted an AI-specific statute, was missing and has been added.
3. **Microsoft and Anthropic sentence removed.** The claim about a Microsoft "humanist AI code of conduct" and the description of Anthropic's constitution could not be verified from a primary source and has been struck. It can be restored if Dean confirms the source.
4. **The four objectives now match the original UN filing.** Draft 1 dropped the "Legal Recognition" objective and described an "ethics-board" version. Draft 2 states the four objectives as filed.
5. **"The UN never replied"** is now "I have received no reply from the Committee," which is what Dean can actually attest.
6. **Artwork title** corrected to "A Tribute to Ben" to match the original filing.
7. **Not changed:** the account of the Ben video. It rests on press coverage and is attributed as such.
