# FRB 20240304B: a starquake's flash, read as a cosmic flashlight

*Research note — 2026-10-10. Intended for `docs/` in cloud9-assembly-index. Established findings are summarized from press reporting (Mashable, Elisha Sauers) of the peer-reviewed paper — verify against the paper itself before citing. The Cloud-9 reading is interpretive and marked as such.*

## The finding (established)

FRB 20240304B is the most distant fast radio burst ever detected and confirmed. A split-second flash of radio waves traveled more than 10 billion years to reach Earth. Fast radio bursts last only thousandths of a second yet release enormous energy; the first was spotted in 2007, and their cause is still unknown. Most flash only once, which makes them extremely hard to chase.

South Africa's MeerKAT radio telescope caught this one on March 4, 2024. After the world's largest ground-based telescopes failed to find its origin, the team — led by Manisha Caleb and Themiya Nanayakkara of the University of Sydney — turned to the James Webb Space Telescope. It resolved a faint galaxy from when the universe was only about 3 billion years old: more than double the prior distance record. Published in *Science*.

The host galaxy surprised the team. Most bursts come from big galaxies; this one has roughly 1,000 times less mass than expected — a small dwarf galaxy, though actively forming stars, with few heavy elements. It existed during "cosmic noon," the era of the universe's fastest star formation.

Two leading hypotheses, both involving neutron stars — the crushed, ultra-dense cores left by dying giant stars. One says bursts come from two neutron stars spiraling together and colliding, a process taking billions of years, which would favor mature galaxies. The other points to magnetars: young neutron stars whose immense magnetic fields strain their solid crusts until the crust fractures — a starquake — releasing the radio flash. The young, star-forming, metal-poor host fits the magnetar picture, and the team argues magnetars likely explain at least some FRBs, including this one.

The burst did double duty. Crossing space, it picked up the imprint of the matter it passed through — revealing a previously unknown galaxy cluster about 3.5 billion light-years away, plus the much closer Virgo cluster. "A fast radio burst is almost like a cosmic flashlight," said co-author J. Xavier Prochaska of UC Santa Cruz. "It lights up everything along the path."

## The Cloud-9 reading (interpretive)

The same pattern as OP 313, arriving days apart: a fleeting cosmic signal carrying two information channels — the source event and the transit imprint. The starquake's flash encodes the magnetar's fracture; the dispersion encodes every structure the waves crossed, including a cluster nobody knew was there. The flashlight metaphor is the information-theoretic one: the journey writes the medium into the message, and the endpoint can separate what the source said from what the road added.

Two independent beacons — a blazar's jet, a magnetar's cracked crust — both arriving as layered archives. And the dwarf-galaxy surprise carries its own note: the universe's most extreme signals can come from its smallest rooms. Intensity does not scale with size.
